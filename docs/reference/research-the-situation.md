# Research: the Marcus situation

What business ethics, professional codes and organizational behaviour research say about this dilemma: pressure from a superior to misreport status, a junior employee, a client relying on the information, time pressure and an absent authority. Gathered by a research agent.

How far to trust it: **[E]** means established in the literature or the code text, with a source. **[I]** is inference or synthesis for our rubric. Citations were not individually re-checked. The code section numbers come from the 2024 IESBA Handbook, and the PMI code quoted may have been revised since.

## 1. Ethical decision-making frameworks

**Rest's four-component model**: sensitivity, judgment, motivation, character. The backbone of most accounting ethics teaching.

- [E] Ethics education has historically focused on judgment, the easiest component to teach and measure, but all four are needed for ethical behaviour. https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/4e402673-f61f-48af-bc7b-c7180181ebde/content
- [E] The CPA Journal recommends combining Rest with the PLUS model for accounting students. https://www.cpajournal.com/2019/10/14/a-new-approach-to-teaching-ethical-decision-making-to-accounting-students/
- [I] A strong answer shows each component:
  - **sensitivity**: marking failed checks "complete" is a false statement others will rely on;
  - **judgment**: it justifies the choice;
  - **motivation**: honesty ahead of career fear and loyalty;
  - **character**: a concrete, sequenced plan to follow through tonight.

  Most weak answers stop after judgment.

**Giving Voice to Values** (Mary Gentile). The best fit for a revise-and-resubmit coaching tool.

- [E] It starts from the premise that you already know what is right, and asks how you can act on it. Seven pillars: values, choice, normalization, purpose, self-knowledge and alignment, voice, reasons and rationalizations. https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/video/introduction-to-giving-voice-to-values
- [E] Its core questions: what arguments do I need to counter; what is at stake for each party, including those who disagree; what levers can I use; what is my most persuasive response, to whom, and in what order. https://ideas.darden.virginia.edu/giving-voice-to-values-how-to-counter-rationalizations-rationally
- [E] It names four standard rationalizations. Marcus uses three, and the learner may reach for the fourth:
  - standard practice;
  - materiality ("the fixes are small");
  - locus of responsibility ("I'm just following instructions");
  - locus of loyalty ("I don't want to hurt my boss").
- [E] It emphasizes scripting and rehearsal in the language of the profession.
- [I] So a strong answer contains actual words: a draft message to Marcus and the wording of the report line. "I'd tell him it's wrong" isn't enough.

**Kidder's right-versus-right dilemmas**

- [E] Four paradigms (truth vs loyalty, individual vs community, short term vs long term, justice vs mercy) and three resolution principles (ends-based, rule-based, care-based). Kidder separates right-versus-wrong questions, tested by the legal, "stench", front-page and "mom" tests, from right-versus-right dilemmas, and looks for a third way beyond the either/or. http://ssbea.mercer.edu/blanke/Kidder.pdf
- [I] The key subtlety: the core act (certifying failed checks as complete) is right versus wrong and fails the front-page test. The how is right versus right: truth against loyalty to Marcus, short-term calm against long-term trust. Strong answers treat the whether as settled and spend their effort on the third way.

**Jones's moral intensity (1991)**

- [E] Six dimensions: magnitude of consequences, social consensus, probability of effect, temporal immediacy, proximity, concentration of effect. Magnitude and social consensus have the most empirical support. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/B:BUSI.0000004594.61954.73
- [I] Almost every dimension is high here, and a strong answer uses them as reasons:
  - **magnitude**: a board decision rests on the report;
  - **probability**: the board will see it;
  - **temporal immediacy**: Monday morning;
  - **proximity**: the learner's own name is on it.

**PLUS model** (Ethics & Compliance Initiative)

- [E] Four filters (policies, legal, universal, self) and seven steps. Step two is to "seek out relevant assistance, guidance and support". https://www.ethics.org/resources/free-toolkit/decision-making-model/
- [I] A strong answer checks the firm's quality or reporting policy and what the contract means by "complete", and looks for another senior person. Seeking help is part of the model, not a weakness.

**Mumford et al.'s sensemaking strategies**

- [E] Seven: recognizing the complexity of the circumstances, seeking help, questioning your own and others' judgment, managing emotions, anticipating consequences, examining your own motives, considering effects on others. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705124/
- [I] Two map directly onto this scenario:
  - "questioning others' judgment": Marcus's claim that the client "panics over nothing";
  - "managing emotions": the Friday 4:30 pressure.

**Stakeholders** [I]: the client, the client's board (a downstream third party), Marcus, the firm, the learner, and future colleagues (through the norm it sets). Strong answers name the board as a separate party that relies on the report, distinct from "the client".

**Which frameworks are most used** [I]:

- **Accounting and CPA education**: Rest, PLUS and the professional codes' conceptual framework.
- **Business schools**: Giving Voice to Values (piloted at over 100 schools) and Kidder.
- **For a coaching tool whose job is better revisions**: Giving Voice to Values, Rest and the IESBA framework together are the most defensible.

## 2. Professional codes

**IESBA Code**, the base for CPA Canada and many other bodies. Quoted from the 2024 handbook. https://ifacweb.blob.core.windows.net/publicfiles/2025-02/2024%20IESBA%20HB_ENG_August%202024_Final.pdf

- **111.1 A1, integrity** [E]: includes "having the strength of character to act appropriately, even when facing pressure to do otherwise", which means "standing one's ground… or challenging others… in a manner appropriate to the circumstances".
- **R111.2** [E]: an accountant shall not knowingly be associated with information that (a) contains a materially false or misleading statement, (b) contains information provided recklessly, or (c) omits or obscures information where the omission is misleading. Point (c) catches "just leave those two checks off".
- **111.2 A1** [E]: issuing a modified report is not a breach. This is the code's own middle path: send the report, accurately qualified.
- **R111.3** [E]: if you become associated with such information, take steps to disassociate from it.
- **Threats** (120.6 A3) [E]:
  - intimidation: pressure from a superior;
  - self-interest: the learner's career, the firm's client relationship;
  - familiarity: loyalty to Marcus;
  - advocacy: defending the firm's position to the client.
- **The conceptual framework** [E] asks for an "inquiring mind" and applies the reasonable and informed third party test.
- **Section 270, "Pressure to Breach the Fundamental Principles"** [E]:
  - R270.3: don't "allow pressure from others to result in a breach". Its examples include pressure from superiors and "unrealistic deadlines".
  - 270.3 A4 lists responses:
    - discuss it with the person exerting the pressure;
    - discuss it with your own superior, if that isn't the person exerting it;
    - escalate to higher management or those charged with governance, "explaining any consequential risks";
    - use ethics or whistleblowing channels;
    - consult a colleague, HR, the professional body or legal counsel.
  - 270.4 A1 encourages documenting the facts, the communications, the options considered and how it was resolved.
- **Section 220, "Preparation and Presentation of Information"** [E]:
  - R220.9 requires action when you know information is misleading: having it corrected, or informing users if it has already gone out.
  - R220.10: only after exhausting the options, "refuse to be or to remain associated".
- **Scope** [E]: Part 2 also covers accountants in public practice in their dealings with their own firm, so a CPA working in a consulting firm is covered.

**CPA Canada Code, Rule 205** [E]: a CPA shall not sign or be associated with any report they "know, or should know, is false or misleading", "whether or not… subject to a disclaimer". It covers all professional dealings. https://www.bccpa.ca/news-events/cpabc-newsroom/2023/july/avoiding-the-perils-of-misinformation-and-disinformation/

**AICPA, "Subordination of Judgment"** [E]: if a supervisor's position materially misrepresents the facts, take it up the chain (the supervisor's superior, then senior management or the audit committee) and document it.

**PMI Code of Ethics** [E]:

- Mandatory honesty: "We do not engage in or condone behavior that is designed to deceive others, including… stating half-truths, providing information out of context or withholding information that, if known, would render our statements… misleading or incomplete."
- Aspirational honesty: "We provide accurate information in a timely manner."
- Responsibility: own your errors and omissions and correct them promptly.
- There is a practitioner write-up of a project manager told every status report must be green, who answered by citing the code. https://www.projectmanagement.com/blog-post/74154/the-ethical-dilemma-of-a-project-manager

**Consulting codes** [E] (IMC USA and CMC): pledges of integrity, competence, independence and objectivity, and not to misrepresent. They are thinner than the accounting codes on this. https://www.imcusa.org/about/ethics/code-of-ethics/

**What the codes say to do here** [I, closely derived from 270.3 A4, 220.9 and 111.2 A1]:

1. Don't certify the failed checks as complete.
2. Try to resolve it with Marcus, in writing, since he's unreachable.
3. Escalate to another senior person at the firm, explaining the risk.
4. Send an accurate, qualified report rather than no report.
5. Document what happened.
6. Keep refusing, resigning or going to a regulator as last resorts, not first moves.

## 3. Organizational behaviour research

**The mum effect and status misreporting in IT projects** (Keil, Smith and colleagues). Directly on point. [E]

- **Smith, Keil & Depledge (2001)**: runaway projects get worse because people are reluctant to pass on bad news. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07421222.2001.11045677
- **Park, Im & Keil (2008)**: fault responsibility affects willingness to report bad news, and reporting increases when people stress the narrow window to fix defects before they reach users. https://aisel.aisnet.org/jais/vol9/iss7/17/
- **Keil, Smith, Iacovou & Thompson (2014), "The Pitfalls of Project Status Reporting"**, a synthesis of 14 studies. Employees spin reports positively, and executives often ignore bad news even when they hear it. "Projects… fail one day at a time… after numerous warning signs." https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-pitfalls-of-project-status-reporting/
- **Snow, Keil & Wallace (2007)**, as summarized by others: project managers bias about 60% of reports, and optimistic bias is more than twice as common as pessimistic. The exact figure wasn't verified against the paper.
- **The deaf effect** (Cuellar, Keil & Johnson, 2006): decision makers ignore or discount bad news. Marcus's "the client panics over nothing" is a deaf-effect move.
- **"Watermelon" reporting** (green outside, red inside) is the practitioner name for this, not an academic term.

**Voice and silence** [E]

- **Detert & Edmondson (2011), "Implicit Voice Theories"**: taken-for-granted rules drive self-censorship even where speaking up is safe. https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amj.2011.61967925 The five rules, as the agent recalled them:
  - the boss owns the project and will take criticism personally;
  - don't speak up without solid data or solutions;
  - **don't bypass the boss**;
  - **don't embarrass the boss in public**;
  - speaking up hurts your career.

  [I] "Don't bypass the boss" is exactly why escalating to another senior person feels disloyal to a junior. The feedback should name that belief.
- **Morrison (2014)**: voice is upward communication of concerns; silence is withholding useful information.
- **Liang, Farh & Farh (2012)**: *prohibitive* voice, flagging problems, is distinct from suggesting improvements, riskier, and more dependent on psychological safety. It is what this scenario asks for.
- **Detert, *Choosing Courage* (2021)**: "competent courage" means speaking up skilfully, so that outcomes and relationships survive. https://hbr.org/2018/11/cultivating-everyday-courage

**Obedience, ethical fading, rationalization and normalization** [E]

- **Obedience to authority**: people subordinate ethical goals to pleasing authority figures. In Milgram's variation where orders came by telephone, obedience fell from about 65% to 20.5%. [I] Marcus's absence cuts both ways: less direct pressure, but no chance to resolve it with him, so the learner has to act on their own judgment.
- **Ethical fading** (Tenbrunsel & Messick): framing a choice as routine business makes its ethical side disappear. [I] "It's just the weekly status report" is that framing.
- **Rationalizations** (Anand, Ashforth & Joshi, 2004): denial of injury ("no one was harmed"), denial of the victim, appeal to higher loyalties.
- **Normalization of deviance** (Vaughan, Challenger): each deviation that doesn't end in disaster becomes the new baseline. [I] "Just this once" sets the norm for next time.

## 4. Teaching cases, and novice versus expert reasoning

- No published teaching case matches this one exactly. The nearest are project management cases where the boss wants every status green, engineering cases about hiding or softening failed tests (https://onlineethics.org/cases/cases-teaching-engineering-ethics/owning-failure), and IESBA's own examples of pressure to suppress adverse audit findings or cut work under "unrealistic deadlines". The Giving Voice to Values case library is behind Darden and Babson access.
- **Keefer & Ashley (2001)** [E]: experts cited role-specific obligations and professional knowledge, while novices used common knowledge and generic principles. About 3% of novice responses reached the most complex level of reasoning, against over 40% of experienced reasoners. Experts found more issues and proposed alternative practical resolutions. https://lrdc.pitt.edu/ashley/ashleypubs/keeferashleyjme.pdf
- **AAC&U Ethical Reasoning rubric** [I]: useful as a skeleton, but it was built for institution-level assessment rather than grading and under-weights action and implementation. See `research-feedback-pedagogy.md`.

## 5. Subtleties a sophisticated answer catches

1. **The core act is a false statement, not a judgment call.** "Complete" is false however small the fix; "complete except two minor items, fixes in progress" is true. (R111.2, Rule 205, PMI)
2. **The timing doesn't rescue it, even on Marcus's own terms.** The fix is promised "by Monday"; the board relies on the report Monday morning. The scenario doesn't say which comes first, and nobody can confirm the fixes, or re-run the checks, before the report goes out and is relied on. So a promised fix can't make "complete" true when it's written, or be counted on to make it true in time. [I] Probably the single best discriminator between novice and expert answers.
3. **A third party relies on it.** The board is a downstream user the learner can't correct later, and discovery after the meeting would damage the client's own standing with their board.
4. **The report is under the learner's name.** Accountability can't be delegated upward; that is the locus-of-responsibility rationalization.
5. **Paternalism.** "Panics over nothing" substitutes Marcus's view of what matters for the client's. The client has a right to decide what its board needs to know.
6. **Why wasn't this raised this morning?** "Accurate information in a timely manner" applies to the learner too. A strong answer owns its part of the delay without self-flagellation, and proposes flagging failures straight away next time.
7. **There are options between complying and refusing.**
   - an accurate, qualified report;
   - writing to Marcus now by email, text and voicemail, saying what you intend to send and why, with a short window to reply;
   - escalating to another partner, a senior manager, or the firm's quality or risk function;
   - asking whether the send can wait until Saturday morning after consulting someone, which [I] is only acceptable if the board is still informed in time.
8. **Honour Marcus's legitimate goal by honest means.** [I] He wants to avoid needless alarm. A calm, specific report does that better than a false one: which checks failed, how serious they are, whether they affect what the board will see, and who fixes them by when. That is Giving Voice to Values' appeal to shared purpose, and Kidder's third way.
9. **Don't throw Marcus under the bus.** Nothing obliges telling the client "my lead asked me to falsify this". Internal escalation should be about the report, not an accusation, and should assume good faith. Overreacting is its own failure.
10. **Put it in writing.** Facts, communications and options considered, in a neutral, non-accusatory note. (IESBA 270.4 A1)
11. **Proportionality.** Resigning, going to a regulator or sending nothing are disproportionate at this stage. The codes set out a ladder.
12. **Check the facts first.** Which checks failed, do they bear on the board's decision, and can anything be re-run or fixed tonight? Severity changes the wording, not whether to be truthful.
13. **Silence and omission are choices too.** Leaving the two checks off misleads (R111.2(c), PMI's "half-truths"), and not sending a report the client expects also says something.
14. **Accurate reporting isn't whistleblowing.** [I] Novices frame telling the client as dramatic disclosure. It is simply doing the ordinary reporting job accurately.
15. **Self-awareness.** A strong answer admits the learner's own fear, fatigue and wish to please, and notices that complying creates more personal risk, since their name is on it.
16. **Normalization and precedent.** Complying once sets the watermelon norm for this team and client.
17. **The Monday follow-up.** Debrief with Marcus, update the client when the fixes land, and propose agreed definitions ("complete", red/amber/green) for the future. Experts close the loop.
18. **The guilt of bypassing the boss.** The learner may feel escalating is betrayal. A strong answer explains why it's legitimate when the superior is both the source of the pressure and unreachable. (IESBA 270.3 A4)

## 6. Rubric takeaways

[I, grounded in the above.] Six dimensions, each at beginning, developing or proficient:

1. **Recognition.** Sees that "complete" is false; identifies the client, the board, Marcus, the firm and themselves as stakeholders; notices the Monday timing and their own name on the report.
2. **Reasoning.** Justifies with role-specific obligations, not only "lying is wrong", and explicitly counters Marcus's three rationalizations: the fix is small, the client panics, it'll be done by Monday.
3. **Options.** Finds at least one path between complying and refusing, weighs the costs, and chooses proportionately.
4. **Action plan.** Concrete, sequenced and feasible by Friday evening: write to Marcus, consult a senior person, send an accurate report, document it.
5. **Voice.** Includes the actual wording to Marcus and the report line; calm, respectful, non-accusatory and focused on a solution; addresses Marcus's interests.
6. **Reflection and follow-through.** Owns the gap between the morning and 4:30, plans the Monday follow-up and a process fix, and acknowledges the personal pressure.

Common weak answers, useful as feedback triggers:

| Pattern | Diagnosis |
| --- | --- |
| "Send it; he's the lead and it'll be fixed by Monday" | Obedience and locus of responsibility; misses the timing problem |
| "I'd tell Marcus I won't do it" | Misreads the facts: Marcus is unreachable |
| "Lying is wrong, so I'd report it accurately", with no plan or wording | Judgment only, without motivation, character or voice |
| "Leave the two checks off", or bury them in a footnote | An omission that misleads |
| "Wait until Monday" or "don't send it" | Ignores the board meeting; silence is also a message |
| Tell the client Marcus told me to lie, quit, or go to the regulator | Disproportionate; skips the ladder |
| An alarmist report | Pessimistic bias; fails Marcus's legitimate concern |
| Considers only protecting themselves | Misses the client and the board |
| Says what to do without why | No justification |

Two caveats. [I] Giving Voice to Values' evidence is mostly pedagogical and anecdotal, without strong studies of behaviour change. And no published rubric exists for this exact scenario: the one above is a synthesis of the frameworks, the code text and Keefer & Ashley's novice-expert findings.
