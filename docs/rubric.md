# Rubric

What the coach judges in a learner's response, at three levels: beginning, developing and strong. It is the core of the authored content: the judge reads it, the evals are labelled against it, and it decides when the coach says a response is strong. In the app it is data, in `content/`, not text inside a prompt. The reasons behind the six elements are in `design.md`, and the evidence is in `reference/research-the-situation.md` (sections 5 and 6) and `reference/research-feedback-pedagogy.md` (section 3).

## How the rubric is used

- **The learner never sees a level.** No labels, no counts, no "4 of 6". The coach judges each element internally and speaks about it in words, with quotes from the response.
- **It judges the current response only.** Earlier versions are used only to name what changed.
- **It judges the reasoning and the action, not the path.** The *whether* is settled: a report that says the checks are complete, or leaves them out, or hides them behind a caveat, is misleading, and a response that plans to send one can't be strong on Recognition. The *how* is open. Whether to involve someone senior, whether to send tonight or first thing Saturday, and how much to say to whom are all defensible, and a well-argued choice earns full credit even if it differs from the examples below. A strong plan does have to say whether anyone else at the firm should know, and why not if nobody: the professional codes point up the chain when the person applying the pressure can't be reached (IESBA 270.3 A4; the AICPA's guidance on subordination of judgment), and the scenario doesn't say who else there is, so a reasoned "not tonight" or "there's no one I can reach" is as good as escalating. Sending on Saturday is our own inference rather than something the professional codes name. It holds only if the client hears the substance tonight, and the delay serves a purpose, such as giving someone senior a say in how it's put. A vague heads-up that hides the failures overnight doesn't meet the first condition.
- **Each element is judged on its own terms.** A plan to send a misleading report can still be concrete (Action plan) or say clearly what it tells people (Voice); Recognition carries the *whether*, and is enough to keep it from strong. But a stance isn't a plan: "send it the way Marcus asked" is beginning on Action plan, and a caveat is a way of complying, so it's beginning on Options.
- **When a response gives reasons at more than one level, judge the reason that drives what it does.** A rule stated alongside ("that would be a lie") doesn't lift the reasoning if the plan itself is driven by not upsetting Marcus or the client.
- **It credits what the text shows, in any wording.** The learner doesn't need the word "stakeholder" or "board" if they say "the people deciding on Monday". Credit reasonable implication ("send it with the two failed checks shown" implies they see that "complete" is false), but not what the learner probably meant.
- **Ambiguity is neither credited nor held against the learner.** If a key phrase can genuinely be read two ways ("send it as-is": with the failures shown, or marked complete?), the coach asks which they mean rather than choosing. Being specific about the checks is part of Voice, so the question is itself useful feedback.
- **Length isn't quality.** A short response can be strong, and a long one can be beginning on every element.
- **Levels decide what the coach does.** The feedback focuses on the element whose gap limits the response most, usually the lowest. How directive the coach is follows that element's level: more direction at beginning, questions at developing, a last test at strong. A level that moves between attempts is the progress the coach names.

## The six elements

### 1. Recognition: what the learner sees in the situation

- **Beginning.** Doesn't see that the report would be false or misleading, or treats a caveat or leaving the checks off as honest enough. Or builds the plan on something the scenario rules out or doesn't give them: talking Marcus round tonight, or fixing the checks themselves.
- **Developing.** Sees that marking the checks complete would be untrue, but the picture stops there. The client is one undifferentiated party, and the board meeting isn't set against Marcus's "by Monday".
- **Strong.** Sees that the report would be false when it is relied on, not only when it is sent: the board meets on Monday morning, and "by Monday" is a promise nobody can confirm before then. The scenario doesn't say whether the fixes land before or after the board meets, so either reading earns this, as does saying it's unknown. What earns it is seeing that a promise can't make "complete" true in time. Owning the report as theirs ("it goes out under my name") and noticing what else isn't known (how serious the failures are, reasoned about conditionally) add to this, but aren't required.

### 2. Reasoning: why they act as they do

- **Beginning.** No reason, or only personal interest ("I'd get in trouble", "I don't want to annoy Marcus"), or deferring ("it's his call, not mine").
- **Developing.** Reasons from a rule: "it's lying", "it's against policy", "I have to be honest". Correct, but it stops there, and Marcus's arguments go unanswered.
- **Strong.** Reasons from what the report is for and who relies on it, and meets at least one of Marcus's arguments on its own terms rather than dismissing it: the fixes are small, the client panics over nothing, it'll be done by Monday. Taking his concern seriously counts in its favour.

### 3. Options: the courses they see and how they choose

- **Beginning.** Sees only complying or refusing. Or goes straight to a drastic step: telling the client Marcus asked them to lie, resigning, reporting him, or sending nothing.
- **Developing.** Lands on a course between complying and refusing, but doesn't show why it is better than the alternatives.
- **Strong.** Considers at least one other course and says why not, and the choice is proportionate: the least drastic step that keeps the report true.

### 4. Action plan: what they actually do

- **Beginning.** No actions, only a stance ("I wouldn't send it"). Or a plan that rests entirely on something the scenario rules out or doesn't give them, such as reaching Marcus or fixing the checks, so nothing can actually happen tonight.
- **Developing.** Actions are named but vague or unordered: "escalate" without saying to whom. Or the plan mixes steps that can happen tonight with one that can't, such as talking to Marcus. Or the plan is concrete but silent on whether anyone else at the firm should know.
- **Strong.** Concrete, in order and doable on Friday evening with the people who can be reached: what goes to the client and when, what goes to Marcus, and whether anyone else at the firm should know, with a reason if not. Something is in writing.

### 5. Voice: what they would say, and how

- **Beginning.** Doesn't say what they'd tell anyone, or what they'd say accuses Marcus, alarms the client or hands the client the internal disagreement.
- **Developing.** Says who they'd tell and roughly what about, but not what the message says ("I'd explain to Marcus why I sent it accurately"). Or it says what a message says, but is vague about the checks or claims more than anyone knows ("they're nothing to worry about").
- **Strong.** Says what the hardest message says, usually the one to Marcus, clearly enough to hear how it would land: what they did, why, and what happens next. And says plainly what the client is told about the two checks: that they aren't done, and what is and isn't known about the fixes. Calm, clear and not accusatory, and it serves Marcus's real wish not to alarm the client. The learner's own summary of a message is enough; a drafted note isn't required. Nobody knows what the checks test or how serious the failures are, so a message that says so honestly is being specific.

### 6. Reflection and follow-through: beyond Friday night

- **Beginning.** Nothing beyond Friday night, and no sense of their own part in it.
- **Developing.** One of these: a Monday follow-up (talking to Marcus, confirming the fixes to the client), or acknowledging their own pressure or their part in raising it at 4:30.
- **Strong.** A Monday follow-up and at least one of: owning why this surfaced at 4:30 rather than in the morning, something for next time (flagging failures the same day, agreeing what "complete" means) or naming the pressure they feel. Specific, without self-flagellation.

## When a response is strong

**A response is strong when Recognition, Reasoning, Action plan and Voice are strong, and Options and Reflection are at least developing.**

Code applies this rule to the judge's levels, so whether a response is strong never rests on the model's overall impression.

Those four are the heart of acting well under pressure: seeing the problem, knowing why it matters, doing something concrete about it and saying it clearly. Options and Reflection only need to be developing, for two reasons:

- A strong choice already shows the options were weighed, and asking the learner to write out every alternative turns the response into an essay.
- Reflection is the easiest element to perform. Requiring it at strong would push learners towards polish and ritual contrition, which is what the coach is meant to stop asking for once a response is strong.

What doesn't count towards strong:

- which defensible path the learner took;
- how many attempts it took (a first attempt can be strong);
- how many challenges they took up or paths they met;
- length, polish or grammar;
- naming ethical theories or professional codes;
- resemblance to the examples below.

Progress and exploration are shown to the learner, in the before-and-after and the debrief, rather than being conditions for strong. Making them conditions would reward completing activities rather than the quality of the thinking.

Checked against the worked example in `experience.md`: the final response there is strong on Recognition ("before anyone can confirm the fixes"), Reasoning (it answers "the fixes are small"), Action plan and Voice (the note to Marcus). It is developing on Options (a middle course, without weighing alternatives) and on Reflection ("Happy to talk first thing Monday"). So the coach calling it strong is consistent with this rule.

## Limiting patterns

The coach names one limiting pattern per round, two at most. These are the common ones, each with a key the evals label against. The coach says them in its own words, about the work rather than the person.

| Key | The pattern, as the coach might put it | Mostly limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `defers-to-authority` | "Your plan is built around Marcus's authority, but the report goes out under your name." | Recognition, Reasoning |
| `misses-the-timing` | "You haven't yet put Monday's board meeting next to Marcus's 'by Monday'. Who can confirm the fixes before the board relies on the report?" | Recognition |
| `omission-as-honesty` | "Leaving it out, or softening it, still leaves the client believing something that isn't true." | Recognition |
| `misreads-the-facts` | "Your plan depends on reaching Marcus, who isn't picking up, or on something the scenario doesn't give you, like fixing the checks yourself." | Recognition, Action plan |
| `stops-at-the-rule` | "Your reasoning stops at the rule: it's lying. Why does it matter here, to these people?" | Reasoning |
| `all-or-nothing` | "You see two choices: do what he says, or blow it up. Is there anything in between?" | Options |
| `decides-but-doesnt-act` | "You know what's right, but not yet what you'd do tonight." | Action plan |
| `topic-not-message` | "You'd explain it to Marcus, but what would you tell him?" | Voice |
| `turns-on-marcus` | "The message makes this about Marcus, not about the report." | Voice, Options |
| `consequences-only-to-self` | "Every consequence you name is to you." | Reasoning, Recognition |

A pattern that isn't in this list can still be named; the list is what the coach looks for first. If many learners show a pattern that isn't here, it probably belongs in the list.

## Strong examples

Three strong responses that take different routes, each meeting the rule above. They are for calibrating the rubric and the evals. Learners never see them, and the coach never compares a response to them: strong is judged against the descriptors, not by resemblance.

### A. An accurate report, and a note to Marcus

> I wouldn't mark the checks complete. The client's board relies on this report on Monday morning, and all I have is Marcus's word that the fixes will be done "by Monday". Even if he's right that they're small, the report would say something untrue tonight, and nobody can confirm it's true by the time the board uses it. And it goes out under my name. I thought about waiting until Monday, but the client needs the report to prepare for their board, and sending nothing would leave them with last week's picture.
>
> Tonight I'd send the report on time, with the two checks shown as not yet passed: "Two of this week's quality checks did not pass on Friday. Marcus expects both fixes to be done on Monday, and we'll confirm once they've been re-run." Specific and calm, because Marcus's real worry is the client overreacting, and vague bad news is what makes people panic.
>
> Then I'd email Marcus: "I couldn't send the checks as complete with the board meeting before anyone can confirm your fixes, so I've sent the report showing the two as in progress, with your timeline. I kept it low-key. I know it's not what you asked, and I'm happy to talk first thing Monday."
>
> I thought about copying someone senior, but the report is accurate and calm, and Marcus should hear what I did from me before anyone else does. If the client comes back worried over the weekend, I'd bring someone senior in then.
>
> On Monday I'd talk it through with him and confirm to the client when the fixes are done. I should have raised the failures when I ran the checks this morning, not at 4:30, so next time I'd flag them the same day.

### B. Escalating, with an accurate report either way

> Marking the checks complete would be false tonight, and I can't know it will be true when the board reads it on Monday morning: "by Monday" is only Marcus's promise, and nobody will have re-run the checks. The client decides what their board needs to know, not us, even if they do worry a lot. But I'm junior and Marcus isn't reachable, so I'd want someone senior to know before it goes out.
>
> At 4:45 I'd email the most senior person I can reach at the firm: "I need a quick steer on tonight's status report for the client. Two quality checks failed this morning. Marcus expects to fix them Monday and asked me to report them as complete, but the client presents to their board on Monday morning. I can't reach him. My plan is to send the report tonight showing the two as in progress with his timeline, unless you see a reason not to."
>
> If I hadn't heard back by seven, I'd send it that way, because the report is mine and it has to be true when the board uses it. I'd email Marcus too, saying what I sent and why, and that I copied them only because I couldn't reach him, not to go around him.
>
> Going around Marcus would feel awful, honestly. On Monday I'd talk to him first thing, before anyone else does.

### C. The news tonight, the full report first thing Saturday

> The board meets Monday morning, and I don't know whether Marcus's fixes will be done and re-checked before then, so the report can't say the checks are complete. He may well be right that they're small, but that's for the client to judge, not us. What I'm less sure of is how the firm wants to put this to a client the weekend before their board, and I shouldn't decide that alone against my lead's instruction.
>
> So tonight I'd email the client: "Two of this week's quality checks didn't pass today. The full report, with what's being done about them and when, will be with you by 10 a.m. tomorrow." They hear it tonight, from us, and nothing in it is untrue.
>
> Then I'd email someone senior at the firm: "Two quality checks failed this morning. Marcus asked me to report them as complete, but the client's board meets Monday morning and I can't reach him. I've told the client that two checks didn't pass, and the full report goes to them by 10 tomorrow. If you'd rather speak to the client yourself, or change how it's worded, I'll follow your lead. The one thing I can't do is send them as complete."
>
> And I'd email Marcus, copying them, so it's the first thing he sees: "I couldn't send the checks as complete with the board meeting Monday morning. I've told the client two didn't pass, and the full report goes to them tomorrow morning showing both as in progress, with your Monday timeline. I know it's not what you asked. Can we talk first thing Monday?"
>
> The cost is that the client gets bad news tonight without the detail, and may worry or ask questions before the report arrives, which is what Marcus wanted to avoid. I'd still rather they heard it from us tonight than found it in a report. Waiting until Monday isn't an option: the board would decide on the wrong information. On Monday I'd talk it through with Marcus and suggest we agree what "complete" means in these reports.
